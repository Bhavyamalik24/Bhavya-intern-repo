# Working with Vulnerable Populations - Reflection

## Research Summary

### Who are vulnerable populations in digital spaces?
Vulnerable populations include people with ADHD, Autism, anxiety, depression,
learning disabilities, and other conditions affecting executive functioning.
In digital spaces, they may face challenges such as:
- Sensory overload from cluttered or busy interfaces
- Difficulty with unclear or inconsistent navigation
- Frustration from unexpected changes or error messages
- Struggling to initiate or complete tasks due to executive dysfunction
- Feeling misunderstood or unsupported by generic productivity tools

### Ethical considerations when designing for neurodivergent individuals
- Never design features that exploit impulsivity or attention difficulties
- Avoid dark patterns (e.g. guilt-tripping language, manipulative notifications)
- Respect user autonomy — give control over settings, reminders, and intensity
- Use plain, clear language — avoid jargon or ambiguous instructions
- Test with neurodivergent users, not just neurotypical ones
- Do not make assumptions about what users "should" find easy

### Making content more accessible for ADHD and Autism
- Use simple, direct language with short sentences
- Break tasks into small, manageable steps
- Use predictable, consistent navigation — no surprise UI changes
- Reduce cognitive load by avoiding too many choices at once
- Provide clear feedback when an action is completed
- Allow customisation of notifications and reminders to suit individual needs
- Use calming, neutral colours and avoid flashing or jarring animations

### Supporting neurodivergent team members
- Be explicit in communication — don't rely on hints or implied expectations
- Put instructions in writing rather than only verbally
- Respect different working styles and time preferences
- Avoid last-minute changes to schedules or plans where possible
- Create a psychologically safe space where people can ask questions freely
- Check in regularly without micromanaging

## Reflection

### How I will adjust my communication style
I will aim to be direct and specific in all written communication — avoiding
vague requests like "can you look into this?" and instead saying "can you review
section 3 of the report and let me know if the data looks correct by Thursday?"
I will also avoid sarcasm in text, which can be easily misread, and always
confirm understanding rather than assuming.

### UX or communication pitfalls that could make Focus Bear less accessible
- Notification overload — too many reminders can cause anxiety rather than motivation
- Vague error messages that don't explain what went wrong or how to fix it
- Onboarding that moves too fast or has too many steps without progress indicators
- Using guilt-based language like "you missed your streak again" which can be
  demotivating for users who already struggle with self-criticism
- Inconsistent UI patterns that require users to relearn navigation

### One practical change I can make in my work
When writing any documentation, reports, or communications related to Focus Bear,
I will apply a "plain language" check — reading it back and asking whether someone
with executive functioning difficulties could follow it clearly without extra context.

## Task

### First-person account I read
I read community discussions from the ADHD subreddit and blog posts from
neurodivergent writers about their experiences with productivity tools. A common
theme was frustration with tools that feel punishing rather than supportive —
particularly streak-based systems that reset entirely after one missed day,
which can cause users to abandon the tool altogether after a setback.

### Design improvement I identified
Focus Bear could offer a "grace day" or "recovery mode" for streaks, so that
missing one day does not completely reset a user's progress. This would be more
compassionate and realistic for users with ADHD who may have high-variability
days due to their condition.

### Practice response to a struggling user

> **Hypothetical user message:**
> "I can't get myself to start anything today. I've opened the app three times
> and just closed it. I feel like such a failure."

**My response:**

Hi, thanks for reaching out — and please don't be hard on yourself.
What you're describing is really common, especially for people managing
executive functioning challenges. Starting is often the hardest part.

One thing that might help: try setting a one-minute task. Seriously, just one
minute — open a document, write one sentence, do one stretch. The goal isn't
to be productive, it's just to break the freeze. Focus Bear's mini-sessions
are designed exactly for moments like this.

You're not a failure for having a hard day. You're here, you opened the app,
and that counts. Take it one tiny step at a time.
